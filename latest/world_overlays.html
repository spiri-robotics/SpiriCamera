3. Drawing on things in the world¶

Most HUD widgets sit at a fixed place on the frame: a battery readout in a corner, a heading tape across the top. Some belong on something in the picture: a marker on home, on the region of interest, on where the rangefinder hit, on each detection. Those things are already in SpiriSynq’s frame tree as Positions with poses in ECEF, so a widget can say which object it belongs on, and the camera works out where that is in the picture.

The widget’s SVG never does the geometry. It draws itself around its own centre, and the camera places it. A widget opts in with one declaration in its template, next to its {# object: #} declarations. A widget that doesn’t declare one works as it always has, and costs nothing extra.

Declaration

Draws

Template gets

{# anchor: <alias> #}

one copy, centred on the object

view: the object’s place

{# anchor: each <alias>.<field> #}

one copy per object linked from <field>

view and item: each linked object’s place and fields

{# project: <alias>, ... #}

one copy, on the frame, as usual

view.<alias>: each object’s place, or none

Every alias still needs its {# object: <alias> = <topic> #} declaration, and a camera can rebind it with overlay_widgets’ "bindings", as for any other widget. A widget has at most one anchor, and either anchors or projects, not both.

3.1. The camera needs a place¶

To know where an object is in its picture, the camera needs its own pose and lens:

  • Its lens is intrinsics, from settings, its source, or SpiriMav (see set_intrinsics).

  • Its pose: a CameraBase is a Position. Mount it on whatever carries it, usually a gimbal, by setting relative_to. SPIRICAMERA_RELATIVE_TO=mu-1/robot_gimbal1 does that for SpiriCamera run, with an identity mount: no offset, the gimbal’s axes. For anything else, set mount over SpiriSynq.

  • A relay (a spirisynq:// source, such as a HUD variant of another camera) shows another camera’s view. It follows that camera’s pose and its lens, live, unless it has been given a place of its own.

Until a camera has both a pose and a lens, it draws no anchored widgets, and view.<alias> in a projecting widget is none.

A camera’s own frame is FRD, like the rest of the tree: x out of the lens, y to the right of the picture, z down it. A camera on a gimbal with an identity mount looks along the gimbal’s x axis, the way a rangefinder on that gimbal points.

3.2. view¶

View’s fields:

Field

x, y

The object’s pixel.

r

The radius, in pixels, of the circle holding the object’s 90% accuracy cylinder (Accuracy), seen from here. none when the object’s accuracy is unknown, or the cylinder reaches behind the camera.

range

Metres from the camera to the object.

edge

Whether the object, or its circle, crosses the frame’s edge.

The units are the ones the widget draws in:

  • For an anchored widget, that means the widget’s own pixels. These are the frame’s pixels when overlays are burned in, and the canvas’s when a browser renders them (render_overlays_for_client). So <circle r="{{ view.r }}"> is the right size either way.

  • For a projecting widget, they are the frame’s pixels, the same as frame.width and frame.height. So the widget covers the frame with width="100%" height="100%" viewBox="0 0 {{ frame.width }} {{ frame.height }}" and draws at the pixels it’s given.

3.3. What gets drawn¶

  • An anchored copy is drawn while any of its circle is on the frame. Off the frame, behind the camera, or with no pose (a rangefinder with no return), it isn’t drawn. A copy partly off the frame is clipped.

  • A projecting widget is always drawn. An object in front of the camera but out of shot still has its pixel, off the frame, and the SVG’s own clipping handles anything drawn there. An object behind the camera, or with no pose, is none. A line to it has no honest 2D end, so skip it:

    {% if view.a and view.b %}<line x1="{{ view.a.x }}" ... />{% endif %}

  • For anchor: each, the field is a list of Links, such as a robot’s gimbals. Each linked object is mirrored as the list changes. A new link shows a frame after the list does, once its object has been mirrored.

3.4. Examples¶

A marker on home, with its distance:

{# object: home = mu-1/robot_home #}
{# anchor: home #}
<svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" width="80" height="44">
  <path d="M40 10 L48 22 L40 34 L32 22 Z" fill="none" stroke="white" stroke-width="2"/>
  <text x="26" y="44" font-family="Miracode" font-size="13" fill="white">
    {{ view.range | round | int }} m
  </text>
</svg>

The widget is 80 px wide and 44 px tall, so its centre (40, 22) is the diamond’s centre, and that is what lands on home.

A ring on each detection, sized to how sure it is of where it is:

{# object: detector = mu-1/detector #}
{# anchor: each detector.detections #}
{% set r = [view.r or 0, 8] | max %}
<svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" width="{{ 2 * r + 4 }}" height="{{ 2 * r + 4 }}">
  <circle cx="{{ r + 2 }}" cy="{{ r + 2 }}" r="{{ r }}" fill="none" stroke="yellow" stroke-width="2"/>
</svg>

A line from where the rangefinder hit to the region of interest:

{# object: spot = mu-1/robot_gimbal1_range #}
{# object: roi = mu-1/robot_roi #}
{# project: spot, roi #}
<svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" width="100%" height="100%"
     viewBox="0 0 {{ frame.width }} {{ frame.height }}">
  {% if view.spot and view.roi %}
  <line x1="{{ view.spot.x }}" y1="{{ view.spot.y }}" x2="{{ view.roi.x }}" y2="{{ view.roi.y }}"
        stroke="white" stroke-dasharray="6 4"/>
  {% endif %}
</svg>

3.5. What it doesn’t tell you¶

  • r is the object’s own uncertainty, not the camera’s. It answers “where in the world could this be, as seen from here”, not “where in this picture will it appear”. These two differ when the object and the camera share an error. A rangefinder spot on the camera’s own gimbal carries the robot’s GPS error in its accuracy, but that error moves the camera too, so in the camera’s own picture the spot sits on the axis however large r is. Draw r when the “where in the world” question is the one the widget answers.

  • The pose is the latest one, not the pose at the instant the frame was taken: SpiriSynq poses carry no time yet. While a gimbal slews, an anchored widget trails the picture by however stale the pose is.

  • The lens is a pinhole, with no distortion. Near the edge of a wide lens, a marker is some pixels off.

  • The camera’s own mount is taken as exact: a camera that is a few centimetres off its gimbal’s axis, or not quite aligned with it, puts everything that far off.
